# Jacobs Inversion

Jane Jacobs' *Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961), Ch. 2, defined the
"eyes on the street" bargain with four terms:

1. Reciprocal — you see the watcher.
2. Local — the watcher lives here.
3. Interventionist — the eye acts.
4. Accountable — to the same block it watches.

Digital eyes invert all four. They are machine, non-reciprocal, itinerant, and
recorded-retained-searchable. Mobile eyes add a fifth failure: **the eye no longer
stays**. A camera that passes through, harvests, and leaves has no stake in the
street.

## Three consequences used in the paper
- A place-based measure cannot see a moving eye. The moving camera has no "where";
  it has a trajectory ([[point-vs-path]]).
- Place-based measures misattribute incidence. A Midtown dashcam is *located* in an
  affluent block group; its *subjects* are outer-borough commuters.
- Jacobs' exit is closed. There is no unwatched route across New York City
  ([[results-equalization]]). The activity-space null measures this.

## Sharp version
Mobile surveillance delivers the optics of Jacobs' eyes while destroying the
reciprocity that made them legitimate — and it distributes those eyes according to
traffic engineering (bus routes, rideshare demand), not residence.
